# 02 — SCRIPT

**THE BET** — a film in nine scenes. Total screen time: ≈30 minutes (1800 seconds).

---

# SECTION A — CHARACTER GUIDE

## PYOTR SEMYONOVICH VOLKOV — "The Banker"

- **Age:** 42 in 1885 (Scenes 1–4 flashback framing); 57 in 1900 (Scenes 5–9).
- **Physical appearance, 1885:** Broad-shouldered, 185 cm tall, heavyset but vigorous. Full dark-brown beard trimmed square, thick dark-brown hair combed back with pomade. Ruddy, well-fed face; small sharp gray eyes; heavy gold signet ring on the right little finger. Moves expansively, laughs loudly, gestures with a cigar.
- **Physical appearance, 1900:** The same man collapsed inward. Beard now iron-gray and untrimmed, hair thin and white at the temples, face sallow with pouches under the eyes, shoulders stooped. Hands tremble slightly. The gold signet ring is loose on a thinner finger.
- **Background:** A self-made financier and speculator. In 1885 he owns a country estate outside Moscow, millions in capital, and the confidence of a man who has never lost. By 1900, desperate stock-exchange gambling has destroyed most of his fortune.
- **Personality:** Proud, generous when it flatters him, cruel when contradicted, terrified of humiliation. His cynicism is a performance that fifteen years slowly turn into self-knowledge.
- **Motivation:** In 1885 — to win an after-dinner argument at any price. In 1900 — to escape paying two million rubles he no longer has.
- **Arc:** From swaggering certainty ("the death penalty is more humane") → to dread → to the decision to murder → to shattering self-contempt when he reads the letter → to a hollow survival, locking the evidence of a better man in his safe.
- **Relationships:** Morozov's jailer, banker, and would-be murderer; master of the watchman Stepan; host to the journalist Orlov.

## NIKOLAI ANDREYEVICH MOROZOV — "The Lawyer"

- **Age:** 25 in 1885; 40 in 1900 (looks 60).
- **Physical appearance, 1885:** Slender, 175 cm, quick nervous movements. Clean-shaven, pale, with black hair worn slightly long, and burning dark-brown eyes. Cheap but carefully kept black frock coat. Bites his thumbnail when thinking.
- **Physical appearance, 1900:** A skeleton with the skin drawn tight over the bones. Long gray-streaked curls falling past the shoulders, a shaggy gray-brown beard to the chest, yellow-toned sallow skin, hollow cheeks, narrow long hand with thin fingers. He looks two decades older than forty.
- **Background:** A young lawyer of no fortune, ambitious, proud, an idealist who has read more than he has lived.
- **Personality:** Passionate, absolute, incapable of half-measures — the same totality that makes him take the bet makes him, fifteen years later, renounce its prize.
- **Motivation:** At first the two million; then pride; then survival; then knowledge; finally, contempt for everything the money stands for.
- **Arc:** Defiant youth → despairing prisoner → sensualist → scholar devouring six hundred volumes → mystic reading the Gospels → the author of the letter: a man who has seen through the world and walks out of paradise five hours early.
- **Relationships:** Volkov's prisoner and, without knowing it, his judge and redeemer. He never speaks to another character after Scene 1; his only voice afterward is written notes and the final letter.

## STEPAN — "The Watchman"

- **Age:** 66 in 1900 (younger in flashbacks, present at the lodge throughout).
- **Physical appearance:** Small, bent, weather-beaten peasant with a white beard cut round, milky-blue eyes, and enormous knotted hands. Wears a long gray watchman's coat and felt boots.
- **Background:** Has served on the Volkov estate for forty years. Guards the lodge, passes food and books through the window, never speaks to the prisoner (as the agreement requires).
- **Personality:** Loyal, superstitious, quietly pitying. He crosses himself when he passes the lodge at night.
- **Motivation:** Duty; and a peasant's unspoken sympathy for the man in the lodge.
- **Arc:** Silent witness for fourteen scenes-worth of years; in the final scene it is he who brings the news of the escape.
- **Relationships:** Volkov's servant; the prisoner's only human contact, always wordless, always through glass.

## YEVGENY IVANOVICH ORLOV — "The Journalist" (Scene 1 only)

- **Age:** 50 in 1885.
- **Physical appearance:** Thin, elegant, silver-haired, gold pince-nez on a black ribbon, pointed gray goatee. Dove-gray evening tailcoat.
- **Background:** A liberal Moscow journalist, guest at Volkov's party.
- **Personality:** Ironic, urbane, the voice of moderation nobody listens to.
- **Motivation:** Good conversation; he tries and fails to defuse the wager.
- **Arc:** None — he exists to witness the terms of the bet and to say, once, that it is madness.
- **Relationships:** Volkov's guest; sympathetic to young Morozov.

# SECTION B — FULL SCRIPT

## SCENE 1 — THE WAGER

**INT. VOLKOV MANSION — DRAWING ROOM — NIGHT (November 14, 1885)**

**Context and purpose:** Establishes the argument, the two men, and the exact terms of the bet. Everything in the film flows from the words spoken here. Duration: 240 seconds.

A long drawing room blazing with candlelight. A dinner party is breaking up into brandy and cigars: eight GUESTS in evening dress, among them ORLOV. Card tables abandoned mid-game. VOLKOV, 42, stands at the marble fireplace, cigar in hand, master of the room. MOROZOV, 25, sits forward on the edge of a bergère chair, wine glass untouched, listening with visible disagreement.

**ORLOV**
(swirling brandy)
The papers say the new commission will recommend abolishing it altogether. Capital punishment, gentlemen, is finished in Europe.

**VOLKOV**
(cheerfully, blowing smoke)
And Europe is wrong, as usual. I don't approve of the death penalty either — but if I must choose between the executioner and the prison, I say the executioner is the more moral man.

A ripple of laughter and protest from the guests.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
The execution kills a man at once. Life imprisonment kills him slowly. Which is kinder — the one who kills you in a minute, or the one who drags the life out of you for twenty years?

**ORLOV**
Both are equally immoral, Pyotr Semyonovich. Both take what they can never give back. The State is not God.

**MOROZOV**
(quietly, then gathering force)
They are equally immoral — but if I were offered the choice, I would choose the second. Without hesitation.

The room turns to him. Volkov's eyebrows rise, amused.

**MOROZOV (CONT'D)**
To live anyhow is better than not to live at all.

**VOLKOV**
(pointing the cigar at him, delighted)
It's not true! Young man. I'll bet you two million you wouldn't stay in solitary confinement even five years.

**MOROZOV**
(standing, pale)
If you mean that seriously — then I'll take the bet. And I'll stay not five years, but fifteen.

A gasp; someone drops a card. Orlov sets down his glass.

**VOLKOV**
Fifteen! Done!
(turning to the room, arms wide)
Gentlemen — I stake two million!

**MOROZOV**
Agreed. You stake your millions; I stake my freedom.

**ORLOV**
(low, urgent, stepping between them)
Think what you're doing, both of you. Nikolai Andreyevich — for you three or four years would take the best years of your life. For him —
(to Volkov)
— two million is a trifle. This is caprice, Pyotr Semyonovich. The caprice of a well-fed man.

**VOLKOV**
(ignoring him, to Morozov, suddenly intimate, almost tender)
Think better of it, young man, while there's still time. Two million is nothing to me — but you're losing three or four of the best years of your life. I say three or four, because you won't stay longer. And don't forget, my friend — voluntary confinement is far heavier than the compulsory kind. The thought that you have the right to walk free at any moment will poison your whole existence in that lodge. I pity you.

**MOROZOV**
(meeting his eyes)
Keep your pity. Fifteen years. To the day.

Volkov studies him a long moment — then laughs and extends his hand. They shake. The guests crowd around.

**VOLKOV**
(calling over the noise)
The terms! Write them down, Orlov, you're a man of letters. He will live in the garden lodge on my estate. Fifteen years — from midnight tonight, the fourteenth of November, 1885, until midnight, the fourteenth of November, 1900. He is not to cross the threshold. Not to see a living soul. Not to hear a human voice. No letters, no newspapers.

**MOROZOV**
(evenly)
And I may have?

**VOLKOV**
A musical instrument. Books — whatever you ask for. Wine, tobacco, food. You'll write your requests on a note and pass it through the little window, and everything will come to you in any quantity — but only through that window, in silence.

**ORLOV**
(writing, grim)
And if he leaves?

**VOLKOV**
If he violates the conditions — even two minutes before the end — even five minutes — the bet is void, and I am free of the two million.

**MOROZOV**
(taking the pen from Orlov, signing)
Then we understand each other perfectly.

He signs. Volkov signs beneath with a flourish. Morozov sets down the pen, looks around the bright, warm, crowded room — the candles, the wine, the faces — as if memorizing it. He bows slightly.

**MOROZOV (CONT'D)**
Gentlemen. Good night — for fifteen years.

He walks out. Through the tall window behind Volkov we see, far off in the dark garden, the small lit shape of the GARDEN LODGE.

**TRANSITION:** MATCH DISSOLVE — the lit window of the lodge seen from the mansion becomes the same window seen from inside, in daylight.

---

## SCENE 2 — YEAR ONE

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — MAIN ROOM — DAY TO NIGHT (1886)**

**Context and purpose:** The first year of confinement: acute loneliness fought with music and light novels. Establishes the lodge, the window ritual with Stepan, and the note system. No spoken dialogue except a single read-aloud note (V.O.). Duration: 180 seconds.

A single large room: whitewashed walls, low beamed ceiling, an iron stove, a narrow bed, a long oak table, one armchair, and an upright PIANO. One small hinged WINDOW PANEL at face height in the door wall — big enough for a tray, too small for a man. Beside the door, the sealed main entrance.

MOROZOV, 26 now, still recognizably the young lawyer but paler, paces the room's diagonal — eight steps, turn, eight steps. His frock coat hangs on a nail; he wears shirt and waistcoat.

He sits at the piano and plays — loud, defiant, a Chopin polonaise, too fast. He breaks off mid-phrase. Silence presses in. He plays again, softer now, and the music continues under the following:

— At the window panel: the panel opens from outside. The knotted hands of STEPAN place a tray: soup, black bread, a carafe of water. The panel shuts. Morozov doesn't turn around.

— Morozov at the table, writing a note in a firm hand. He passes it through the panel.

**MOROZOV (V.O.)**
(the note, read in his young, steady voice)
"Send novels — with complicated love plots, if you please. Also comedies. Something with crime in it, and something fantastic. No wine: wine excites desires, and desires are the worst enemy of a prisoner. And no tobacco — it spoils the air in the room."

— Books arrive through the panel, a stack of yellow-backed novels. He reads in the armchair, feet on the stove ledge, devouring pages.

— NIGHT. The single kerosene lamp burns on the table. Morozov plays the piano in the dark, only his hands lit. Outside the small window, snow. From the distant mansion, warm lit windows. He stops playing. He rests his forehead against the piano lid. His shoulders shake once — then he straightens, and plays on.

**TRANSITION:** SLOW DISSOLVE through the lodge window — snow melting, leaves, snow again — to the same room five years on.

---

## SCENE 3 — YEAR FIVE

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — MAIN ROOM — NIGHT (1890)**

**Context and purpose:** The despair phase: music silenced, wine embraced, work abandoned. The lowest point of the confinement. Duration: 150 seconds.

The same room, degraded. The piano is SHUT, dust on the lid. Empty wine bottles rank along the wall — dozens. The table is littered with plates, crusts, an overturned inkwell whose stain has dried into the wood.

MOROZOV, 30, heavier-faced, unshaven, lies on the bed in a stained dressing gown, eyes open, staring at the ceiling beam. A full glass of dark red wine stands on a chair by the bed.

He talks to himself — we see his lips move angrily, hear only fragments, half-voiced:

**MOROZOV**
(muttering, to the ceiling)
...and I said to him — I said — fifteen years, to the day... to the day...

He laughs — a short, ugly sound — drinks the glass in one motion, refills it from a bottle on the floor without sitting up.

— LATER: Morozov at the table, writing furiously by lamplight, page after page. He stops. Reads what he has written. His face goes still with disgust.

He gathers the manuscript — twenty, thirty pages — and TEARS IT, methodically, into smaller and smaller pieces, until the table and floor around him are covered with paper scraps like the snow outside.

He sits motionless in the wreckage. Then he lowers his head onto his arms among the torn paper. In the lamplight, he WEEPS — the full, hopeless weeping of a man who believes no one can hear him.

At the window panel, unseen by him: the panel is open a crack. STEPAN's milky eye watches for a moment. The old man crosses himself and gently, silently, closes the panel.

**TRANSITION:** HARD CUT on the sound of a page turning — loud, crisp — to bright daylight.

---

## SCENE 4 — THE HUNGER FOR EVERYTHING

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — MAIN ROOM — DAY (1891–1894) / EXT. GARDEN — DAY**

**Context and purpose:** The great turn: languages, philosophy, six hundred volumes; the six-language letter and the pistol shots; then the Gospels. Shows the prisoner conquering his prison. Duration: 210 seconds.

The same room, transformed again. The bottles are gone. BOOKS have taken the room: stacked on the piano, ranked along the walls in towers, open on the bed. The table is an ordered battlefield — dictionaries, grammars, notebooks, a map of the ancient world pinned to the whitewash.

MOROZOV, mid-30s, gaunt now, hair long, works standing at the table like a surgeon. He writes out Greek verb forms, checks a dictionary, writes again. His lips move — silent languages.

— The window panel opens and closes, opens and closes, in rhythm: books IN, books OUT, notes OUT. Stepan's hands ferry crates of volumes.

— Morozov writes a LETTER — careful, beautiful script, six distinct alphabets visible as he covers the page. He seals it and passes it through the panel.

**MOROZOV (V.O.)**
(the letter, in a voice now deeper, worn, precise)
"My dear jailer. This letter is written in six languages. Show it to men who know them. Let them read it. If they find not one mistake, I beg you — have a shot fired in the garden. That shot will tell me my labors have not been in vain. The geniuses of all ages speak different tongues, but the same flame burns in them all. Oh, if you knew my unearthly happiness, now that I can understand them!"

**EXT. GARDEN — DAY (continuous)**

Wide autumn garden, bare black trees. VOLKOV, 50 now, graying, stands on the gravel path with the open letter in his hand. He looks toward the lodge for a long moment. Then he nods to STEPAN, who raises an old double-barreled shotgun to the sky.

TWO SHOTS — flat, enormous, echoing off the mansion. Rooks burst from the trees.

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — CONTINUOUS**

Morozov stands at the small window, eyes closed, palms flat on the sill, as the echo dies away. On his ruined face: pure, complete joy — the last joy we will ever see there.

— LATER (1894, winter light): The towers of books are gone; the room is bare and clean as a cell. On the table lies ONE BOOK: the New Testament. Morozov reads it slowly, one page per sitting, his finger under the line like a child. Behind him on the wall, where the map was, a small plain wooden cross.

**TRANSITION:** FADE TO BLACK. Fade in on a lamp flame in darkness.

---

## SCENE 5 — THE BANKER'S ARITHMETIC

**INT. VOLKOV MANSION — STUDY — NIGHT (November 13, 1900)**

**Context and purpose:** Fifteen years later, the night before the term expires. Volkov, ruined, works his way from despair to the decision to murder. A monologue scene — the film's dark mirror of Scene 1. Duration: 240 seconds.

A masculine study gone shabby: dark green walls, a vast desk, a cold fireplace, a black iron FIREPROOF SAFE in the corner. Rain lashes the windows. One lamp burns.

VOLKOV, 57, gray and stooped, sits at the desk over a chaos of papers — ledgers, notices from the exchange, unpaid drafts. He computes with a pencil, and the pencil stops.

**VOLKOV**
(to the papers, flat)
Fourteen years ago I had more millions than I could count. Now I'm afraid to ask which I have more of — money, or debts.

He rises, walks to the window, watches the rain. Far across the black garden: the lodge's single lit window.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
That cursed bet. Why didn't the man die? He's forty. He takes my last coin, he marries, he enjoys life, he gambles on the exchange — with my money — and I look on like an envious beggar, and hear the same words from him every day: "I am indebted to you for the happiness of my life. Let me help you."

He turns from the window. His breathing is audible.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
Tomorrow at twelve he regains his freedom. Under the agreement, I must pay him two million. If I pay — I am ruined. Everything is finished.

He stands very still. The clock on the mantel ticks. When he speaks again it is barely a whisper.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
No. It is too much even for me. The one escape from bankruptcy and disgrace — is that the man should die.

He looks at his own hands. He crosses to the SAFE, spins the combination, opens it: inside, bundles of documents and a small SEALED ENVELOPE, wax-stamped, marked in ink: *"КЛЮЧ — LODGE KEY. Sealed 14.XI.1885."*

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
(taking the envelope, steadying his voice like a man rehearsing testimony)
The clock has just struck three. Everyone in the house is asleep. If I do it, suspicion falls first on the watchman. Old Stepan sleeps like the dead.

He breaks the fifteen-year-old seal. The dry wax cracks loudly. He takes out an iron key, pockets it, pulls on his overcoat, and picks up the lamp. At the study door he stops — one look back at the portrait of himself, 1885, ruddy and laughing, above the fireplace — then goes out.

**TRANSITION:** CUT on the sound of the door — to wind and rain.

---

## SCENE 6 — ACROSS THE GARDEN

**EXT. VOLKOV ESTATE — GARDEN — NIGHT (continuous)**

**Context and purpose:** The murder walk. Pure atmosphere and dread; the film's only sustained exterior. Duration: 120 seconds.

Blackness, rain, a keen wind. VOLKOV comes down the mansion steps with a hurricane lamp, coat collar up, and enters the garden. Bare trees heave overhead. He can see neither the ground nor the white statues along the path; he steers by memory.

He stops. Ahead, a dim shape — he raises the lamp: only a statue, a lichen-stained Diana, streaming with rain. He exhales and goes on.

At the KITCHEN GARDEN wall he calls, low:

**VOLKOV**
Stepan!

No answer. Rain on leaves. He tries the door of the watchman's hut beside the wall: inside, glimpsed through the crack, STEPAN asleep under a sheepskin, mouth open, dead to the world.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
(under his breath, to himself)
Asleep. He'll be the first they suspect — if I have the courage to do it.

He wades on through wet drifts of dead leaves — each step a loud, sodden crunch — to the LODGE. The porch. The door, its lock skinned over with fifteen years of paint and rust. He peers through the main window first: inside, a candle burns low on the table; a motionless FIGURE sits bent over it, back to the window. He taps the glass — once, twice. The figure does not stir.

Volkov puts the iron key to the lock. The rusted lock GRINDS — a long, tearing shriek — and the door gives.

**TRANSITION:** CUT to interior on the sound of the door's creak.

---

## SCENE 7 — THE LETTER

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — MAIN ROOM — NIGHT (continuous)**

**Context and purpose:** The climax. Volkov comes to kill, finds the letter, and is destroyed and remade by it. Longest scene of the film. Duration: 330 seconds.

The room by candlelight: bed unmade, the cold stove, the shut piano, one book open on the table beside the candle. At the table, asleep in his chair, head bowed onto his crossed arms: MOROZOV, 40 — a skeleton in a long black coat, gray-streaked curls to his shoulders, shaggy beard, the yellow skin tight on the bones of his face. Before his bowed head, a page covered in fine handwriting.

VOLKOV steps in, lamp lowered. He expects to be heard. He is not — the sleeper does not move. Volkov crosses half the room, stops an arm's length away.

**VOLKOV**
(whisper)
Poor devil. Asleep — and probably dreaming of his millions.

He looks at the bed. He looks at his own hands. We understand the plan without a word: *take the pillow, press it down; the worn body will not fight long.*

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
(whisper, steeling himself)
And I have only to take this half-dead thing, throw it on the bed, a moment's pressure of the pillow — and the most conscientious examination will find no trace of violence. But first —

His eye falls on the PAGE under the candle.

**VOLKOV (CONT'D)**
— first, let us read what he has written here.

He draws the page carefully from under the sleeping man's arms. He tilts it to the candle. MOROZOV'S VOICE takes over — quiet, dry, absolutely calm — as Volkov reads. Throughout the reading we move between the handwriting, Volkov's collapsing face, and the sleeping prisoner.

**MOROZOV (V.O.)**
"Tomorrow at twelve o'clock midnight I regain my freedom, and the right to mix with men. But before I leave this room and see the sun, I think it necessary to say a few words to you. On my own clear conscience, and before God who sees me, I declare to you: I despise freedom, and life, and health, and all that your books call the blessings of the world."

Volkov's hand tightens on the page. The rain ticks on the glass.

**MOROZOV (V.O.) (CONT'D)**
"For fifteen years I have studied earthly life attentively. It is true I saw neither the earth nor its people — but in your books I drank fragrant wine, I sang songs, I hunted deer and wild boar in the forests, I loved women... Beauties, light as clouds, created by the magic of your poets, visited me by night and whispered wonderful tales that made my head drunk. In your books I climbed the peaks of Elbrus and Mont Blanc, and saw from there how the sun rose in the morning, and in the evening flooded the sky, the ocean and the mountain ridges with crimson gold. I saw green forests, fields, rivers, lakes, cities. I heard sirens singing, and the pipes of shepherds. I touched the wings of beautiful demons who flew down to me to talk of God..."

Volkov sinks slowly onto the edge of the bed, still reading. The lamp trembles in his other hand; he sets it on the floor.

**MOROZOV (V.O.) (CONT'D)**
"Your books gave me wisdom. Everything that unwearying human thought created through the centuries is pressed into a small lump inside my skull. I know I am cleverer than you all. And I despise your books. I despise all the blessings of this world, and all its wisdom. Everything is void, frail, ghostly and deceptive, like a mirage. You may be proud, wise and beautiful — but death will wipe you from the face of the earth like the mice under the floor; and your descendants, your history, your immortal geniuses will freeze or burn together with the terrestrial globe."

The sleeping man breathes — one long, rattling breath. Volkov flinches, frozen. Morozov does not wake. Volkov reads on.

**MOROZOV (V.O.) (CONT'D)**
"You have lost your reason and gone the wrong way. You take lies for truth, and ugliness for beauty. You have exchanged heaven for earth. I do not want to understand you."

**MOROZOV (V.O.) (CONT'D)**
"That I may show you in deed my contempt for that by which you live — I renounce the two million, of which I once dreamed as of paradise, and which I now despise. That I may deprive myself of any right to it, I shall leave this place five hours before the time fixed — and so break the agreement."

The page drops a centimeter in Volkov's hands. He reads the last lines again with his lips moving. Then he lays the sheet gently on the table.

Volkov stands. He looks down at the sleeping skeleton — the man he came to smother. He bends and KISSES the top of the strange, gray-streaked head — once, softly, like a father.

**VOLKOV**
(barely voiced, wrecked)
Forgive me.

He begins to weep — silently, catastrophically, a man who has not wept in fifty years — picks up the lamp, and goes out, leaving the door ajar behind him. Through the doorway: the rain, the dark. The candle flame bends in the new draft. The sleeper sleeps.

**TRANSITION:** SLOW DISSOLVE to the study.

---

## SCENE 8 — THE SLEEPLESS HOUSE

**INT. VOLKOV MANSION — STUDY — NIGHT TO PRE-DAWN (continuous)**

**Context and purpose:** The aftermath of the letter: Volkov's self-contempt, the sleepless remainder of the night. Quiet beat before the resolution. Duration: 120 seconds.

The study door opens; VOLKOV enters soaked, sets the lamp down, and does not take off his coat. He sits on the edge of the daybed — not the desk chair — hands hanging between his knees.

**VOLKOV**
(to no one; the only sound apart from rain)
At no other time — even after losing everything on the exchange — have I felt such contempt for myself as I feel now.

He puts his face in his hands. Hold.

— TIME CUT, marked by the light: the windows go from black to slate-gray. Volkov lies on the daybed in his coat, eyes open, not sleeping. The clock hands stand at a quarter past six. The rain has stopped; water drips from the eaves, slower, slower.

From far off in the house: RUNNING FOOTSTEPS, growing, then a fist hammering on the study door.

**STEPAN (O.S.)**
(through the door, breathless)
Master! Pyotr Semyonovich! Master!

Volkov sits up.

**TRANSITION:** HARD CUT to the door bursting open.

---

## SCENE 9 — FIVE HOURS EARLY

**INT. VOLKOV MANSION — STUDY — MORNING / EXT. GARDEN & GARDEN LODGE — MORNING (November 14, 1900)**

**Context and purpose:** Resolution. The escape is confirmed; Volkov locks the letter in the safe. Final images: the empty lodge, the empty road. Duration: 210 seconds.

The study door bursts open. STEPAN, 66, hatless, mud to the knees, gray with agitation, stumbles in and crosses himself.

**STEPAN**
Master — the man from the lodge! We saw him climb out of the window into the garden! He went to the gate — and off down the road! Gone!

Volkov stands. A long beat: on his face, not shock — something like awe.

**VOLKOV**
(quietly)
Come.

**EXT. GARDEN — MORNING (continuous)**

Volkov and Stepan cross the garden fast through the wet leaves — the same path as in the night, now drab gray daylight, mist in the black branches. The lodge ahead: its casement WINDOW stands wide open, the curtain hanging out over the sill, soaked.

**INT. GARDEN LODGE — MAIN ROOM — MORNING (continuous)**

Volkov steps in first. The room, in daylight, for the last time: the chair pushed back from the table, the bed untouched since night, the candle burned to a puddle. No one.

Stepan hovers at the threshold — after fifteen years, still afraid to cross it.

**STEPAN**
(whisper, crossing himself)
Fifteen years, master... and he leaves five hours before the time. Why would a man do it?

Volkov doesn't answer. On the table: the LETTER, exactly where it was laid. He takes it, folds it once, and puts it inside his coat, over his chest.

**VOLKOV**
(without turning)
Say nothing of what you saw, Stepan. To anyone. The man in the lodge has forfeited the bet — that is all anyone needs to know.

**STEPAN**
(bowing his head)
As you say, master.

**INT. VOLKOV MANSION — STUDY — MORNING (later)**

Volkov alone. He stands before the open SAFE. He looks one last time at the letter in his hands — the fine handwriting, the six-language discipline in every line — then places it inside, on top of the dead documents of his fortune, and swings the iron door shut. The LOCK turns with a heavy triple clank.

He remains standing before the safe, hand flat on the cold iron, head bowed — the exact posture of the sleeper at the lodge table.

**EXT. ESTATE GATE AND ROAD — MORNING**

The last image: the estate gate standing open. Beyond it, a straight dirt road running to a flat gray horizon between bare fields. The road is EMPTY. Wind moves the puddles. Hold twelve seconds.

**FADE TO BLACK.**

**TITLE CARD (white on black):** *"To live anyhow is better than not to live at all." — the lawyer, 1885*

**END OF FILM.**
